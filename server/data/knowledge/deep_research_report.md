# SME Loan Information and Related Data in Sri Lanka

## SME definition and classification in Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka’s most widely cited “policy” definition of micro, small, and medium enterprises is set out in the National Policy Framework for SME Development published by the entity["organization","Ministry of Industry and Commerce","sri lanka govt ministry"]. It defines SMEs using **two criteria**—**annual turnover** and **number of employees**—and explicitly differentiates the employee thresholds for the **manufacturing** and **services** sectors. citeturn44view0

**Policy definition (National SME Policy Framework):**
- **Micro**: turnover **< LKR 15 mn**; employees **< 10** (manufacturing and services). citeturn44view0  
- **Small**: turnover **LKR 16–250 mn**; employees **11–50** (manufacturing and services). citeturn44view0  
- **Medium**: turnover **LKR 251–750 mn**; employees **51–300** (manufacturing) and **51–200** (services). citeturn44view0  

The policy framework also clarifies that the “SME” category (small + medium) is intended to cover enterprises with **<300 employees** and **turnover ≤ LKR 750 mn**, and that **employment** is the deciding factor if turnover and employee thresholds point to different size categories. It also notes that the definition should be revisited periodically. citeturn44view0

In parallel, the entity["organization","Central Bank of Sri Lanka","central bank, sri lanka"] has used **operational definitions** for banking regulation and measurement that are **broader on turnover** than the policy definition. In its 2020 “SME Credit Survey” (introduced to close an information gap on MSME lending), banks were requested to treat MSMEs as establishments with **annual turnover not exceeding LKR 1 billion** and **employment <300**, consistent with certain CBSL directions (as described in the CBSL Annual Report 2020 box). citeturn25view0

This same **turnover ≤ LKR 1,000 mn** criterion is also embedded in the CBSL’s **priority-sector lending targets** for MSMEs (Monetary Law Act Order No. 01 of 2021), which explicitly states that enterprises with annual turnover not exceeding **LKR 1,000 mn** will be considered MSMEs for that Order. citeturn42view1turn42view0

**Why definitions matter for SME lending:** eligibility thresholds, reporting to regulators, portfolio segmentation (e.g., “SME desks”), and pricing metrics such as the Average Weighted SME Rate (AWSR) are tied to the operational “MSME” perimeter used by banks and regulators. citeturn25view0turn36view0turn42view1

## Loan providers and major public programs

Sri Lanka’s SME-finance ecosystem is primarily bank-led, with important complementary roles for government interest-subsidy schemes, microfinance institutions, and non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs). The CBSL’s SME Credit Survey itself is framed around lending by **licensed commercial banks (LCBs)** and **licensed specialised banks (LSBs)**. citeturn25view0

The government’s interest-subsidy umbrella program “Enterprise Sri Lanka” and related concessionary schemes are implemented through a mix of state and private banks. ### Government-supported lending programs

**Enterprise Sri Lanka (interest-subsidy schemes).** A central design feature is that the government pays part of interest to participating banks, yielding an “on-lending” rate to borrowers that is below market rates. For example, the concept paper provides loan limits and on-lending rates for some flagship schemes (e.g., “Jaya Isura,” “Green Loan,” etc.), with differentiated categories by SME size and sector. citeturn43search5

**SME Line of Credit (SMELoC) funded by ADB.** The entity["organization","Asian Development Bank","multilateral development bank"] describes Sri Lanka’s SMELoC as a line of credit delivered through **13 participating financial institutions** to targeted SMEs. citeturn43search4turn45search19

**Samurdhi community banking / microfinance channel.** Parliamentary material on the Samurdhi system indicates an islandwide network of community banks/bank societies and describes lending under multiple loan schemes for livelihood-related credit needs. It reports that the total value of loans granted under 21 loan schemes during 2024 was **LKR 54,197.04 mn (≈ LKR 54.2 bn)**. citeturn43search12  
Separately, the Samurdhi Department hosts circulars on revising annual interest rates of Samurdhi loan schemes, indicating an administered loan-rate framework in that channel. citeturn43search0turn43search26

## Loan products, pricing, and typical terms

### Common product types used for SMEs

The CBSL’s MSME priority-sector lending order explicitly defines eligible “credit facilities” broadly to include **term loans, leasing, overdrafts, and trade finance facilities (including off-balance-sheet exposures)**, denominated in both local and foreign currency, granted to MSME-sector individuals and businesses. citeturn42view1  

In practice, SME lending in Sri Lanka often clusters around four use-cases:
- **Working capital** (overdrafts/revolving lines; factoring; short-tenor trade/invoice finance). citeturn42view1turn45search0turn45search6turn45search14  
- **Capex / expansion** (term loans; SMELoC investment loans; leasing/asset finance). citeturn42view1turn43search4turn43search31  
- **Sector programs / policy-linked lending** (Enterprise Sri Lanka category schemes tied to sectors and interest subsidies). citeturn43search2turn43search5  
- **Micro and nano-enterprise lending** (microfinance loans; Samurdhi channel). citeturn39view0turn43search12  

### Interest rates and pricing signals

A key advantage in Sri Lanka relative to many markets is that the CBSL publishes dedicated **MSME lending-rate indicators**:
- **AWSR (Average Weighted SME Rate)**: weighted average interest rate on *all outstanding* rupee loans to MSMEs by licensed banks.
- **AWNSR (Average Weighted New SME Rate)**: weighted average interest rate on *new MSME loans granted in a given month*.
These rates exclude government refinance schemes and exclude non-performing loans, and are computed from the CBSL SME survey. citeturn36view0

**Latest published SME rate snapshot (as of Nov 2025):**
- AWSR: **11.43%**
- AWNSR: **10.92%** citeturn36view0turn36view3  

**Historical reference (end 2020):**
- AWSR at end 2020: **10.95%**
- AWNSR for December 2020: **8.53%** citeturn25view0  

This suggests that, at least for bank-credit captured in the CBSL MSME survey perimeter, **typical effective bank SME rates have been in the ~9%–12% band in the cited periods**, while still varying meaningfully by loan size, borrower quality, and institution type (LCB vs LSB). citeturn25view0turn36view0

**Concessionary policy-rate examples** exist under interest-subsidy schemes. For instance, Enterprise Sri Lanka’s Jaya Isura design sets subsidised on-lending rates of **6.75% (Category I)** and **10.12% (Category II)** in its concept documentation. citeturn43search5  
A bank implementation page from the Regional Development Bank also publishes these same Category I/II rates and provides indicative tenors and grace periods. citeturn43search16

### Tenor, grace periods, and collateral patterns

Tenor and collateral vary by product category, but some program documents provide concrete parameters:
- Under RDB’s “Jaya Isura,” the maximum repayment period is **5 years**, including a **12-month grace period**, and security may include personal guarantees and movable/immovable assets. citeturn43search16  
- For DFCC’s SMELoC tea-smallholder product, the bank advertises repayment up to **10 years** and a minimum **24-month grace period** for new planting/replanting, with indicative loan size bands tied to grant financing. citeturn43search32  

At the system level, the CBSL has explicitly noted that Sri Lanka’s lending constraints for MSMEs have been associated with heavy reliance on collateral and a risk-averse culture in institutions, combined with information asymmetries and MSME-side weaknesses (e.g., transparency and financial literacy). citeturn25view0

## Eligibility, application pathways, and bottlenecks

Sri Lanka does not operate with a single unified SME-loan “application” regime; instead, eligibility is shaped by (i) the SME definition used by the lender/product, (ii) sector/program rules, (iii) credit-risk policy and collateral constraints, and (iv) any regulatory reporting perimeter.

### Eligibility criteria patterns in published programs

A concrete, currently published example is DFCC’s SMELoC eligibility filter:
- registered SME,
- turnover **< LKR 1,000 mn**,
- employee threshold **<200** (manufacturing or services in DFCC’s published criteria),
- exclusions for **trading, leasing, and renting** businesses (as stated by DFCC for that scheme),
- and a women-ownership pathway that requires **majority women ownership (51%)** or a multi-criterion alternative. citeturn43search8  

On the regulatory side, the CBSL’s MSME priority-sector order defines the eligible MSME perimeter via **turnover ≤ LKR 1,000 mn**, and specifies that credit facilities counted can include term loans, leasing, overdrafts and trade finance. citeturn42view1

### Common bottlenecks highlighted by Sri Lankan authorities

Rather than prescribing “standard timelines,” Sri Lankan official publications often emphasise *structural and information bottlenecks*:
- The CBSL described an **information gap** on MSME credit volumes and rates as a constraint for policymaking, which led to the introduction of its monthly SME Credit Survey in July 2020. citeturn25view0  
- The CBSL (citing ADB) also identifies three binding constraints: (1) lender-side constraints (risk aversion and collateral dependence), (2) market infrastructure gaps (information asymmetry), and (3) MSME-side limitations (financial literacy and transparency). citeturn25view0  

Together, these issues help explain why, even when program funds exist (e.g., concessional lines of credit), SMEs may still face uneven approval outcomes across banks and across borrower segments. citeturn25view0turn42view1

## Regulatory and policy environment shaping SME finance

### Priority sector lending targets for MSMEs

Sri Lanka implemented a formal, CBSL-mandated MSME **priority sector lending target** via Monetary Law Act Order No. 01 of 2021. Key features:
- **Target form:** licensed banks must ensure a **growth rate of not less than 20% per annum (YoY)** in the outstanding stock of MSME lending over the previous year. citeturn42view0  
- **MSME perimeter:** enterprises with annual turnover **≤ LKR 1,000 mn** are considered MSMEs for the Order. citeturn42view1turn42view0  
- **Product breadth:** term loans, leasing, overdrafts and trade-finance exposures can be included, but **pawning advances** are explicitly excluded from counting toward the target. citeturn42view1turn42view0  
- **Reporting:** banks must report MSME lending details quarterly to the CBSL. citeturn42view1turn42view0  

This is a distinctive institutional feature for SME credit in Sri Lanka because it operates as a **regulatory credit-growth floor** for MSME lending rather than only an interest-subsidy incentive. citeturn42view0

### Microfinance interest rate regulation

Under the Microfinance regulatory regime, the CBSL previously imposed a **maximum effective annual interest rate** cap for licensed microfinance company (LMFC) microfinance loans:
- Microfinance Act Direction No. 2 of 2019 set a ceiling of **35% per annum (effective annual interest rate), inclusive of all other charges** for microfinance loans granted by LMFCs. citeturn37search1  
- In June 2022, the CBSL (Monetary Board) **revoked** this maximum-interest-rate Direction and shifted to monitoring microfinance lending through a reporting mechanism, with the option to take action if “exorbitant” rates are charged. The CBSL letter also defines the monitored microfinance loan perimeter as unsecured loans **≤ LKR 250,000**, granted for income-generating activities or certain consumption purposes linked to income-generating microfinance activity. citeturn39view0

### Credit infrastructure reforms and secured lending

Sri Lanka has also been implementing credit-infrastructure reforms intended to expand access to finance by strengthening collateral frameworks and data systems. The entity["organization","International Finance Corporation","world bank group arm"] highlighted the launch/effectiveness of a **Secured Transactions Registry** as an initiative expected to expand credit access for MSMEs and support inclusive finance. citeturn45search15

### Capital-market and alternative finance: equity crowdfunding direction of travel

Sri Lanka’s entity["organization","Securities and Exchange Commission of Sri Lanka","capital markets regulator"] has run public consultations and drafted rules for facilitating **equity crowdfunding**, including draft rules for equity crowdfunding intermediaries/market operators (consultation-stage documents). citeturn45search9turn45search1turn45search13turn45search17  
While this does not yet equate to mainstream SME financing volumes, it signals a regulatory pathway for non-bank, capital-market-linked SME fundraising tools. citeturn45search9turn45search1

## Challenges, innovations, and case-based insights

### Core barriers SMEs face in accessing finance

Sri Lankan official analysis points to a triad of barriers that mirrors what many SME markets face, but with acute intensity during crisis and post-crisis periods:
- **Collateral dependence and risk aversion** within financial institutions. citeturn25view0  
- **Information asymmetries** (limited reliable data to assess MSME borrowers), explicitly cited as a policymaking and credit-allocation challenge, motivating the CBSL’s SME Credit Survey. citeturn25view0turn36view0  
- **MSME-level governance and capability constraints**, including financial literacy and transparency. citeturn25view0  

Credit quality stress also matters for forward-looking SME credit supply. The FSR data show high and rising NPL ratios for MSME-purpose loans (25.2% → 30.5% across Q2-22 to Q2-23), which is consistent with lenders tightening underwriting and insisting on collateral or stronger cash-flow evidence after macro shocks. citeturn31view0turn28view0

### Digital and analytic innovations influencing SME credit

Several developments align with “data-driven” SME lending and risk management:
- CRIB reports a migration to an advanced IT platform and increased digitalisation of services, enabling more seamless access to credit information. citeturn45search3turn45search7  
- Commercial Bank announced an AI-powered SME credit underwriting solution leveraging machine learning and predictive analytics for SME needs assessment and underwriting support. citeturn45search23

These innovations are consistent with a broader trend toward **faster, data-enabled underwriting**, though their systemic reach depends on adoption across lenders and on data coverage quality. citeturn45search7turn45search23

### Alternative financing instruments that function as “SME liquidity rails”

Even when term credit is constrained, Sri Lankan SMEs can access liquidity through transaction-backed instruments:
- **Factoring / invoice discounting** (examples: LOLC Finance, Singer Finance, Dialog Finance, Siyapatha Finance). citeturn45search6turn45search14turn45search4turn45search16  
- **Supply chain finance and invoice finance** offerings (example descriptions from Standard Chartered). citeturn45search2turn45search0  

These tools are especially relevant for SMEs integrated into larger buyer–supplier networks, because underwriting can partly rely on the strength of invoices and buyer relationships rather than only SME collateral. citeturn45search2turn45search14

### Public program outcomes as “case evidence” at portfolio level

Program reporting offers measurable outputs that can be interpreted as portfolio-level “success signals,” even when firm-level case studies are not disclosed:
- One year (2023) performance reporting shows sizable disbursement counts and volumes through SMELoC and Jaya Isura (thousands of loans and tens of billions of LKR), indicating scale and uptake. citeturn43search13  
- A later report indicates continued SMELoC disbursement in 2024, including investment loans to SMEs through PFIs. citeturn43search31turn43search4  

A useful “lesson” from microfinance policy evolution is that interest-rate caps were once formalised (35% effective APR) but later revoked with a shift toward monitoring and enforcement actions against “exorbitant” rates—suggesting that regulators have balanced consumer protection goals against market-functioning concerns and enforcement practicality. citeturn37search1turn39view0
